<h2 id="section-2">Analyse</h2>
<h3 id="section-3">Key Points</h3>
<p><strong>1. Three Strong Primes:</strong> The primes <img alt="𝑝" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.645" src="formula-04-01.svg" style="width:0.7072em;height:1.1175em" width="9.901"/>, <img alt="𝑞" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.645" src="formula-04-02.svg" style="width:0.5456em;height:1.1175em" width="7.638"/>, and <img alt="𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-04-03.svg" style="width:0.5397em;height:1.1071em" width="7.556"/> used to define the finite
fields are strong primes chosen from secure cryptographic sources. These
primes are also pairwise co-prime, which is important for the next step in
solving the challenge using the Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT).</p>
<p><strong>2.  CRT  Application:</strong>  Since <img alt="𝑝" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.598" src="formula-04-04.svg" style="width:0.6628em;height:1.1142em" width="9.280"/>, <img alt="𝑞" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.598" src="formula-04-05.svg" style="width:0.5435em;height:1.1142em" width="7.609"/>,  and <img alt="𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-04-06.svg" style="width:0.5412em;height:1.1071em" width="7.577"/>  are  pairwise  co-prime,  we  can
apply the Chinese Remainder Theorem to reconstruct <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-04-07.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> modulo <img alt="𝑝 ∗ 𝑞 ∗ 𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.571" src="formula-04-08.svg" style="width:3.5104em;height:1.1122em" width="49.146"/>.
This property allows us to uniquely determine <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-04-09.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> because the product <span class="inline-math-scroll pdf-math-sequence" tabindex="0"><img alt="𝑃 [0] ∗" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.325" src="formula-04-10.svg" style="width:2.6786em;height:1.1661em" width="37.500"/><img alt="𝑄[0] ∗ 𝑅[0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.298" src="formula-04-11.svg" style="width:4.7262em;height:1.1641em" width="66.167"/></span> will be smaller than <img alt="𝑝 ∗ 𝑞 ∗ 𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.516" src="formula-04-12.svg" style="width:3.5179em;height:1.1083em" width="49.251"/>, making <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-04-13.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> recoverable.</p>
<p><strong>3. XOR Masking:</strong> The script adds <img alt="𝑅[0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.333" src="formula-04-14.svg" style="width:1.9643em;height:1.1666em" width="27.500"/> to <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-04-15.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> and then XORs the result
with the flag. Once <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-04-16.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> is recovered, reversing the XOR operation will allow
us to recover the original flag.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">How to solve</h2>
<h3 id="section-5">Recovering <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="18.167" src="formula-05-01.svg" style="width:0.6786em;height:1.2976em" width="9.500"/></h3>
<p>To solve this challenge, we need to reverse the process:</p>
<p><strong>1.</strong> The first step is to recover <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-05-02.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> modulo <img alt="𝑝" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.601" src="formula-05-03.svg" style="width:0.6972em;height:1.1143em" width="9.761"/>, <img alt="𝑞" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.601" src="formula-05-04.svg" style="width:0.5478em;height:1.1143em" width="7.670"/>, and <img alt="𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-05-05.svg" style="width:0.5529em;height:1.1071em" width="7.741"/>. These values are given
as <code>F1(s)</code>, <code>F2(s)</code>, and <code>F3(s)</code> in the output.</p>
<p><strong>2.</strong>  Since <img alt="𝑝" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.609" src="formula-05-06.svg" style="width:0.6911em;height:1.1149em" width="9.675"/>, <img alt="𝑞" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.609" src="formula-05-07.svg" style="width:0.5357em;height:1.1149em" width="7.500"/>,  and <img alt="𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-05-08.svg" style="width:0.5374em;height:1.1071em" width="7.524"/>  are  prime  and  co-prime,  we  can  apply  the  Chinese
Remainder Theorem to reconstruct <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-05-09.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> modulo <img alt="𝑝 ∗ 𝑞 ∗ 𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.581" src="formula-05-10.svg" style="width:3.4806em;height:1.1130em" width="48.729"/>. This works because
the values of <img alt="𝑃 [0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.335" src="formula-05-11.svg" style="width:1.9643em;height:1.1668em" width="27.500"/>, <img alt="𝑄[0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.335" src="formula-05-12.svg" style="width:1.9643em;height:1.1668em" width="27.500"/>, and <img alt="𝑅[0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.335" src="formula-05-13.svg" style="width:1.9679em;height:1.1668em" width="27.550"/> are smaller than their respective field
moduli, ensuring that <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-05-14.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> is fully recoverable.</p>
<p><strong>3.</strong> Once we have <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-05-15.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/>, we add <img alt="𝑅[0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.343" src="formula-05-16.svg" style="width:1.9643em;height:1.1674em" width="27.500"/> to match the value before the XOR operation, and then XOR the result with the value stored in <code>s  XOR  flag</code> to

<h3 id="section-7">Explanation</h3>
<p><strong>Chinese  Remainder  Theorem:</strong>  By  leveraging  the  fact  that <img alt="𝑝" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.043" src="formula-07-01.svg" style="width:0.7105em;height:1.1459em" width="9.946"/>, <img alt="𝑞" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.043" src="formula-07-02.svg" style="width:0.5513em;height:1.1459em" width="7.719"/>,  and
<img alt="𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-07-03.svg" style="width:0.5505em;height:1.1071em" width="7.707"/> are co-prime, we can apply the CRT to reconstruct <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-07-04.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> modulo <img alt="𝑝 ∗ 𝑞 ∗ 𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.015" src="formula-07-05.svg" style="width:3.5104em;height:1.1440em" width="49.146"/>,
which directly gives us the value of <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-07-06.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/>. This is feasible because the product
<img alt="𝑃 [0] ∗ 𝑄[0] ∗ 𝑅[0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.742" src="formula-07-07.svg" style="width:7.5357em;height:1.1959em" width="105.500"/> is smaller than <img alt="𝑝 ∗ 𝑞 ∗ 𝑟" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.961" src="formula-07-08.svg" style="width:3.4989em;height:1.1400em" width="48.985"/>.</p>

<p><strong>Recovering the Flag:</strong> After recovering <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-07-12.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/>, we need to add <img alt="𝑅[0]" class="pdf-inline-math" height="16.086" src="formula-07-13.svg" style="width:1.9778em;height:1.1490em" width="27.690"/> to reverse
the addition in the original script. Once done, we can XOR the result with
the masked flag value <code>s XOR flag</code> to recover the original flag.</p>
<p><strong>Note:</strong> To  recover <img alt="𝑅" class="pdf-inline-math" height="12.833" src="formula-07-14.svg" style="width:0.7262em;height:0.9167em" width="10.167"/> <img alt="𝑥" class="pdf-inline-math" height="12.833" src="formula-07-15.svg" style="width:0.5931em;height:0.9167em" width="8.303"/>-coordinate,  (needed  to  recover <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="12.833" src="formula-07-16.svg" style="width:0.4881em;height:0.9167em" width="6.833"/>  before <code>s  +=  int(R[0])</code>),  the

<h2 id="section-8">Conclusion</h2>
<p>This challenge highlights the power of the Chinese Remainder Theorem in
modular arithmetic, especially when working with cryptographic systems
based on finite fields. By correctly reconstructing <img alt="𝑠" class="pdf-inline-math" height="15.500" src="formula-08-01.svg" style="width:0.5833em;height:1.1071em" width="8.167"/> through CRT and reversing the XOR operation, we are able to successfully recover the flag.</p>
